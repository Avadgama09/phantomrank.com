# Hooks Are Great, but Sometimes You Need an Uppercut

For a long time, the advice for writing content was simple:

> **Start with a hook.**

Say something surprising. Ask a question. Create curiosity. Make someone stop scrolling long enough to hear what you have to say.

And honestly, that advice still holds. A hook matters. If nobody starts reading, it does not matter how good the rest of the piece is.

But I think content needs something else now.

**It needs an uppercut.**

- A hook gets attention.
- An uppercut changes mental position.

In boxing, a hook comes from the side. It catches you off guard. You notice it late. It makes you pay attention.

An uppercut comes from below. **It lifts your head.** For a moment, it changes where you are looking.

That is what the best content does.

It does not just make you think, *"That was interesting."* It gives you something you did not have before: a fact, a pattern, an experience, a way of seeing the problem, a piece of evidence that makes the old explanation feel incomplete.

And once you have seen it, you cannot completely go back to seeing the problem the same way.

That is an uppercut.

---

## We Became Obsessed with Hooks

It makes sense that hooks took over content.

Attention is expensive. Everyone is competing with everything else: work, messages, videos, emails, news, notifications, other posts, other tabs, and whatever is happening in real life.

So people learned the formulas:

- *"Nobody is talking about this..."*
- *"Here is the truth about..."*
- *"I wish I knew this sooner..."*
- *"Stop doing this if you want..."*
- *"The biggest mistake people make is..."*

Some work. Some are tired. Most can be spotted from a mile away.

But even a good hook only earns a few seconds. It makes a person pause. It does not automatically give them a reason to care after that.

A post can have a brilliant opening and still leave the reader with nothing new. It can make a strong claim, use the right words, get a few likes, and disappear from everyone’s mind by the end of the day.

The problem is not that hooks are bad. **The problem is when the hook is the most valuable thing in the piece.**

---

## An Uppercut Is Information Gain

When I say "uppercut," I do not mean being louder.

I do not mean pretending to have a controversial opinion. I do not mean writing something provocative just so people argue in the comments.

**An uppercut is new, credible information that changes how someone understands something.**

That "new" part matters. Not necessarily new to the entire world. You do not need to discover electricity every time you write a blog.

But it should be new in a meaningful way:

- New to the reader.
- New to the conversation in your category.
- New evidence that was not easily available before.
- A pattern you noticed through doing the actual work.
- A clearer explanation of something people keep getting wrong.
- A conclusion that comes from experience, not from reading five other blogs and rewriting them.

That is what people mean when they talk about **information gain**.

Information gain is not *more* information. It is information that changes the quality of the answer.

It is the part that makes someone say:

> *"I had not thought about it like that."*

Or:

> *"That explains something I have been seeing but could not put into words."*

Or even:

> *"Okay, we need to change what we are doing."*

That last one is usually the real test.

---

## A Hook Makes You Look. An Uppercut Makes You Look Differently.

Take this sentence:

> *"AI search is changing SEO."*

It is true. It can work as a hook. But it is also something everyone has already heard. An AI model could generate that sentence in half a second. A hundred agencies could put it on their website this week. It does not give anyone a new way to think or act.

Now consider this:

> **"AI visibility is not only a ranking problem. It is a brand-description problem."**

That is different. It makes you ask a different set of questions.

Not just:
- *"Does an AI model mention us?"*
- *"Do we appear when someone asks about our category?"*

But also:
- *"What does the model actually say about us?"*
- *"Does it understand what makes us different?"*
- *"Does it describe us the way our best customers would?"*
- *"Does it recommend us for the right reason?"*
- *"If it finds our website but does not use it as a source, what is missing?"*

The first sentence tells you that a change is happening. The second changes the problem you are trying to solve. That is the difference.

| A Hook | An Uppercut |
|---|---|
| Makes someone pause | Makes someone reconsider |
| Creates curiosity | Creates a shift in understanding |
| Can be copied quickly | Usually comes from real evidence or experience |
| Gets someone into the content | Gives them something to take away from it |
| Says, "Look at this" | Says, "You may be looking at this wrong" |

A good piece of content can have both. It should probably have both.

**But the uppercut is the part people remember.**

## AI Has Made Generic Content Cheaper

This is where AI changes the content conversation.

For years, businesses could publish a perfectly acceptable article simply by covering a topic better than the competition:

1. Find the keyword.
2. Look at the top-ranking pages.
3. Build a better outline.
4. Add a few examples.
5. Include the keyword in the title.
6. Publish.

That process still has value. Clear structure matters. Search intent matters. Technical SEO matters. Useful pages still need to be easy to find and easy to understand.

But there is a problem now. **AI can produce an acceptable version of most generic articles almost instantly.**

- It can write *"10 tips for local SEO."*
- It can explain *"what is AI visibility?"*
- It can draft *"a beginner’s guide to content marketing."*
- It can summarize the same advice that already exists in hundreds of similar pieces.

So if your content is only a tidy rewrite of public information, it is becoming harder to justify why it needs to exist.

This is not an argument that blogs are dead. It is an argument that **interchangeable blogs are becoming less valuable.**

Google’s public guidance around generative AI in Search has made a similar distinction. It emphasizes valuable, people-first, non-commodity content: content that brings a unique perspective, expertise, or experience rather than recycling material that already exists or could be easily generated.

That feels like an important signal.

The opportunity is not just to write content that answers a question. **It is to publish something that makes the answer better because you were involved.**

> **AI can summarize what is already known. Your business needs to publish what it knows that is not already obvious.**

---

## What Businesses Already Have That AI Does Not

Most businesses do not lack content ideas. They lack a way to recognise the valuable material they are already sitting on.

The useful stuff is often not in the marketing folder. It is scattered across the company:

- The questions your sales team hears every week.
- The objections that keep coming up on calls.
- The mistakes customers make before they buy.
- The reason a project took longer than expected.
- The reason another project worked better than expected.
- The detail your team has learned after doing the work fifty times.
- The point where standard advice does not work in the real world.
- The internal spreadsheet nobody has turned into a useful public insight.
- The thing customers misunderstand until they see it explained properly.

That is where uppercuts come from. Not from trying to be impressive—from noticing what you know because you are close to the work.

- A **software company** might have data showing where users get stuck during onboarding.
- A **recruitment agency** might see that clients consistently hire for the wrong skill first.
- A **digital agency** might know that most website redesign projects fail before the design stage because no one agrees on the actual job of the site.
- A **local business** might understand how buyers in Ahmedabad, Mumbai, Surat, or Rajkot make decisions very differently from the generic advice written for a global audience.

That knowledge can become content. And when it is properly explained, it becomes much harder to replace with an AI-generated summary.

## Not Every Uppercut Needs a Huge Study

When people hear "original information," they often imagine a massive survey with 5,000 respondents and an expensive PDF. That is one form of original information. But it is not the only one.

A small, honest observation can be more useful than a large, vague report.

For example:

> *"Most businesses think they need more blog traffic. But after reviewing our last 30 sales calls, we found that buyers were not struggling to find information. They were struggling to compare options once they found it."*

That is not a global research finding. It does not pretend to be one. But it is real. It is specific. It leads to a useful conclusion: maybe the next piece of content should not be another awareness article. Maybe it should be a clear comparison page, a buyer’s guide, a pricing explainer, or an honest breakdown of who the product is not for.

Or consider this:

> *"We assumed people wanted more features. What they actually wanted was one clear answer to whether the tool would work with the systems they already use."*

Again, not a grand theory. Just something learned from real work. That is often enough.

The point is not to publish a blockbuster insight every week. **The point is to stop confusing more words with more value.**

---

## The Question Worth Asking Before You Publish

Before publishing a blog, a LinkedIn post, a landing page, or a guide, ask:

> **Could someone create this with almost the same value just by asking an AI model to summarize the existing internet?**

If the honest answer is yes, that does not mean you should throw it away. It means there is probably more work to do.

Ask what you can add:
- A specific example from your work.
- A customer pattern.
- A real data point.
- A limitation nobody usually admits.
- A before-and-after lesson.
- A screenshot, template, or decision process.
- A comparison that makes a hard choice easier.
- A point of view you can actually explain and defend.
- A question the market has not answered properly.

The goal is not to make every article revolutionary. **The goal is to make sure it leaves a mark.**

---

## The Uppercut Should Come Early

A common mistake in business content is hiding the actual idea.

The writer spends 1,000 words explaining the context, agreeing with everything everyone already agrees with, and setting up the point. By the time they get to the useful insight, most readers are gone.

Do not do that. **If you have an uppercut, bring it in early.**

Start with the familiar idea. Let the reader recognise it. Then show what it misses.

For example:

> *"We have spent years learning how to write hooks because attention is scarce. But AI can now write hooks, rewrite them, test them, and generate thousands of versions of them. The harder thing—and the more valuable thing—is to publish an idea, a piece of evidence, or a pattern that did not exist in the answer before you showed up."*

That gives the reader the point quickly. The rest of the article can explain it, test it, and make it useful. But do not make people work too hard to find out why they should care.

## What This Means for AI Visibility

This is one of the reasons AI visibility should not be treated as a simple rankings game.

There is a big difference between an AI system mentioning a brand and using that brand as a meaningful source.

- A **mention** can create awareness.
- A **citation** can create a path for someone to investigate further.

And there is another useful signal in between: sometimes a system may find a page but not use it in the final answer. That is not proof that the business is invisible. It can mean the page was available but did not add enough clarity, relevance, authority, or useful information for the specific question being answered.

That is a content problem worth understanding.

At **PhantomRank**, we look at AI visibility through separate signals such as mentions, citations, and pages that were retrieved but not used in the final answer. The point is not to promise control over what an AI system says. Nobody can honestly promise that. The point is to help businesses see where their information is missing, unclear, or less useful than the alternatives available to the model.

This is where **information gaps** become practical.

If AI answers in your category are vague, repetitive, outdated, or missing an important distinction, that is not just an SEO opportunity. **It is an opportunity to contribute something useful.**

- Maybe you have the data.
- Maybe you have the practical experience.
- Maybe you have the case study.
- Maybe you have the explanation the category has been missing.

That is the content worth creating.

---

## The Shift Is Simple

Hooks are still useful. Use them. Write better openings. Make your titles clearer. Respect the fact that people are busy. Give them a reason to start reading.

**But do not confuse attention with value.**

- A hook gets someone in.
- An uppercut gives them something they did not have before.

And as AI makes it easier to create competent, generic content at scale, that difference matters more.

The businesses that stand out will not just be the ones publishing the most. They will be the ones publishing things that could only have come from them:

- Their own evidence.
- Their own experience.
- Their own customer patterns.
- Their own way of explaining a problem.
- Their own answer to the question everyone else keeps answering badly.

> **Do not try to add another paragraph to the internet. Add something that changes the answer.**
